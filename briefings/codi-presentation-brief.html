What the deck argues

It answers all five of their questions — but its spine is a claim: a £100m budget under pressure is a decision problem, not a reporting problem.

Rather than describing a process for investigating, it arrives with three evidenced hypotheses and the data test for each. That is the difference between an answer that is competent and one that is remembered. Everything on the slides is framed as a question about Codi, never an assertion about how Codi works.

Three things to check before you send it

  1. Re-read the Yellow line in the judgement PDF. I could not reach gov.wales to verify it independently, so the deck rests on your reading of the primary document. It is the single claim you would least want to get wrong in front of this panel — and the one they will know best.
  2. Say the 23% is UK-wide. Quoting an England-weighted survey as though it were Welsh is exactly the kind of slip a finance panel notices.
  3. The DLO expansion is 2024, not fresh. If someone says “we sorted that two years ago”, the answer is: “Good — then it is not hypothesis one. Which of the other two should I be looking at?” That reply is stronger than the hypothesis was.

A word on the Yellow grade. Raising it is the right call — it shows you have read the current judgement rather than an old one, and it is what makes slide 6 land. But keep it to one flat, factual sentence and move straight to the consequence. Anything more reads as telling them about their own organisation.

03What they are likely to push on

The presentation sits inside a one-hour interview, so expect these to come straight after.

“You have assumed quite a lot about how we operate.”
Agree immediately. They are hypotheses with tests attached, built from public information, precisely because you did not want to assert anything about their operation. Then turn it into a question: which of the three is closest?
“How would you size the overspend without our data?”
You would not — you would decompose it. Volume, price, mix, productivity. Name what you would ask for on day one: the ledger, work orders and commitments, contractor call-off by lot, operative hours, and the stock condition data behind the planned programme.
“What if it is simply demand?”
Then it is a capacity and unit-cost conversation rather than a control one — and the mitigation set changes completely, because you cannot save your way out of volume. That answer shows you are not attached to your own hypothesis.
“Is rephasing not the obvious answer?”
It is the last lever, not the first, and it is rarely a saving. It converts capitalised spend into revenue and tends to raise responsive demand later. Slide 5 already says this — say it again, plainly.
“What would you do in your first 90 days?”
Three things: walk the operation before changing a single report; get one trusted set of repairs numbers; then use it to reopen one decision currently being made on instinct.
Something very specific about their reporting, from Tom Hirst.
If you do not know, say so and ask. In front of the person who does the job, a confident guess is far more damaging than an honest question — and asking well is itself evidence you would be good to partner with.
